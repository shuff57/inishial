Teacher sign-in

Why iniSHial

Everyone on the same page. Literally.

A syllabus is the one document that says what a course expects: how late work is graded, what counts as cheating, what happens after three absences, what a student needs to bring. It is handed out once, in the first week, and then it has to hold for the rest of the year.

The hard part was never writing it. It is knowing that the people it applies to have read it.

What usually happens

The syllabus goes home in a backpack. Some of it comes back. The signature at the bottom says a parent saw the last page, which is not the same as reading the section on late work, and a teacher holding a stack of paper has no way to tell the difference. The families who most need to have read it are the ones whose page does not come back at all.

Then in November there is a conversation about a grade, and it turns on whether anyone knew the policy. Nobody can answer that from a stack of paper.

What this does instead

The syllabus lives on a page a parent can open on a phone. They read it a section at a time and put their initials on each one, not a single signature at the end but a mark against late work, against attendance, against academic honesty, each one next to the policy it belongs to.

The student initials too, separately

Every account has two access codes: one for the parent, one for the student. Which code is used at sign-in is what decides whose initials get written, so they are never the same string. Both parties agree to the same document, and the record says which of them did.

The teacher sees who has, and who has not

One page lists every student on the roster and where they are: complete, partly done, never started, or never registered. Chasing the four families who have not read it is a different job from chasing thirty.

When the syllabus changes

Policies get corrected mid-year. When you publish a new version, everyone who signed the old one is marked as having signed an older version rather than being quietly counted as done. That is deliberate and it is occasionally inconvenient: it means a real change asks people to look again, instead of the record claiming agreement to words nobody saw.

What this is not

It is worth being straight about the limits, because a school will ask.

This is not identity verification. It shows that someone holding the parent's access code read the syllabus and initialed it. That is the same assurance a signed paper form gives you, which is to say a reasonable one and not a legal one. Nothing here proves who was holding the phone.

It does not email anyone. Codes go out through whatever a teacher already uses, so there is no mail provider in the middle and no new place for a roster to leak from.

It is not a gradebook, a messaging app, or a place to put anything else. It does one document. Everything a student or parent can reach is the syllabus and their own initials.

Getting started

Students set up an account with their student ID and school email, and are shown their access code once. Parents use the code their teacher sends them. Teachers import a roster, paste or upload the syllabus they already wrote, and publish it.
